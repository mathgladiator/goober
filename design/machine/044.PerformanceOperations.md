# 044. Performance and operations review (2026-10-31)

A review of the work from 2026-10-29 to 2026-10-31 (commits `bd11d87` to `b9bc422`), for
two questions. Is it fast enough where it runs often? Can the human run the host: start,
stop, upgrade, roll back, see trouble, back up, restore, and keep the disk from filling?
Fixes that were small and clearly right are made and tested. The rest are listed with
reasons. Decisions are left for the human (§ 4).

Severity: **high** means it can hurt the site or lose data; **medium** means it hurts an
operator or will hurt at more scale; **low** is worth knowing.

## 1. Measurements

The worktree's build ran on a 32-core laptop, with a scratch host on its own storage, in
local mode, with the default `threads.network: 2`. The load came from Python `http.client`
with keep-alive (six processes of four threads), so the client caps some of these numbers.

| what | result |
| --- | --- |
| `GET /goober/health`, one process of 8 threads | 15,240 req/s, p50 0.48 ms, p99 1.04 ms |
| `GET /about` (a markdown page), one process | 16,700 to 17,500 req/s, p50 0.43 ms, p99 0.85 to 1.11 ms |
| `GET /no-such-page` (the site's 404), one process | 17,330 req/s, p50 0.42 ms, p99 0.95 ms |
| `GET /about`, six processes at once | about 19,000 req/s in all, p50 1.21 ms, p99 1.86 ms; the two network threads each at 100% of a core |
| access log after about 333,000 requests | 115 MB (about 350 bytes a line) |
| `traffic`, `error_report`, `box_failures`, `uri_tree` (12h, tenant) | 15 to 21 ms each |
| `hot_boxes`, `host_health`, `uri_stats` | 4 to 10 ms |
| `access_log` | 173 ms before the fix in § 2.1, 84 ms after |
| `log_search {}` (the newest 200 lines) over that day's 115 MB file | **5,544 ms** before the fix in § 2.1, **9.6 ms** after |
| `log_search {pattern}` that matches nothing (reads the whole file) | 322 ms after |

A page costs about 100 µs of a network thread. At two network threads that is about 19,000
pages a second on one machine, which is far more than the experiments need. `threads.network`
raises it.

## 2. Fixed

### 2.1 The log dive read a whole day to find its newest lines (high, fixed)

`logs::search` (`host-src/packages/logs/src/search.cpp`) read each file from its first line
and kept the last `limit` matches, so the newest 200 lines of today's access log cost a read
and a JSON parse of every line. On 115 MB that took 5.5 seconds. Every `log_search`,
`access_log`, the panel's Logs view, `agent_audit`, and `version_health` go through it, and
a busy day's file reaches 256 MiB before it rotates.

**Fix:** each file is read backward from its end, in 1 MiB chunks, so a newest-first search
stops once it has `limit` matches. Windows, cursors (`before_*`), `max_read`, the deadline,
and scopes behave as before. A window now stops at its older edge (`since`) instead of its
newer one. **Test:** `Logs.SearchReadsBackOnlyAsFarAsItNeeds`: 20,000 lines over 6 MiB; the
newest 5 read under 64 KiB; every line comes back once, in order, across chunk edges; a
middle window; and a search that crosses into the rotated file.

### 2.2 A crash mid-try left copies of private rows on disk for good (medium, fixed)

`route_try` (`route_try.cpp:243`) and `upgrade_dry_run` copy whole databases under
`storage/tmp/tries/` and `storage/tmp/upgrades/`. They removed those copies when the call
returned, but a crash or kill mid-call left them, and nothing swept them. A job dry run's
copies were already swept at start (`init_jobs`). `upgrade_dry_run` also skipped its removal
when the upgrade threw.

**Fix:** `init_jobs` removes `tmp/tries` and `tmp/upgrades` at startup, before anything runs.
`upgrade_dry_run` removes its copies on every way out, including a throw. **Test:** the
`RouteTryTest.AWriteRunsOnCopiesAndChangesNoRealData` test leaves cut-off directories, starts
a host, and checks that they're gone.

### 2.3 Nothing said the disk was filling (high, fixed in part)

Nothing in the host looked at free space, though a full disk fails every write at once:
databases, the WAL, logs, the outbox, snapshots, and the nightly's copy of storage.

**Fix:** `/goober/ready` (and `host_health`'s `ready`) has a `disk` check with `free_bytes`
and `total_bytes` for the filesystem holding storage. `"low": true` is set under 10% or
2 GiB free, as a warning that doesn't fail the check. Under 256 MiB free the check fails,
since writes are about to fail anyway. HUMAN.md § 1 says what it means and how to find what
holds the space. **Test:** `PanelTest.ReadySaysWhatEverySubsystemIs`. Nobody is told unless
they look; see § 4.1.

### 2.4 Exports could outlive their 7 days (low, fixed)

`exports::list` deleted expired exports, but it ran only when someone made or listed an
export. **Fix:** the hourly housekeeping deletes expired exports too. **Test:** in
`DataRoutesTest.ExportsSealRowsForAPersonToFetch`.

### 2.5 HUMAN.md's export fetch didn't work as written (medium, fixed)

`export get --out /tmp/rows.csv` run as `goober` writes a mode-0600 file that the human's
ssh user can't read, so "copy it off with scp" failed. **Fix:** `--out -` writes the CSV to
standard output, unsealed only for a moment in storage's `tmp` and removed at once, so
`ssh … 'sudo -u goober goober-host export get ID --out - …' > rows.csv` lands it on the
human's own machine. HUMAN.md shows that. **Test:** the export test captures standard output
and checks that nothing is left behind.

- READINESS.md § 8 said traffic history came from "four files of 64 MiB". The logs keep 7

## 3. Found, not fixed

### 3.1 Each access-log line takes a global lock and five system calls (medium)

`logs::Writer::write` (`logs/src/logs.cpp:327`) runs on the network thread for every
request. It holds the writer's mutex and an `flock` while it calls `fstat` and `stat` (the
rotation check), hashes the chain, and calls `write`. That's what lets several processes
share one chain (032 § 1). It serializes all request threads on one lock, and the two
network threads in § 1 saturate near 19,000 pages a second.

It isn't fixed because batching lines, or one writer thread with a queue, changes when a line
is durable and how a second process (the CLI) joins the chain. Worth doing before traffic
nears that.

### 3.2 The traffic tailer's first run reads every log on disk under its lock (medium)

With no cursor (a new install, or a lost `traffic.db`), `Traffic::tail`
(`operations/src/traffic.cpp:362`) reads every rotation, up to 64 files of 256 MiB. It holds
`Traffic::mu_` the whole time, so `traffic`, `error_report`, `uri_tree`, `box_graph`, and the
panel wait on it for as long as it takes, possibly minutes. A fix would read in bounded
slices and release the lock between them, as each batch already commits its cursor.

### 3.3 Tries and dry runs copy whole databases (medium)

`route_try` on a write, `job_dry_run`, and `upgrade_dry_run` each copy
every database the box can write (SQLite's online backup), then count every table's rows
twice. That's fine for today's databases, which are kilobytes to megabytes. A 1 GB database
makes each try a gigabyte of disk and seconds of I/O, and two concurrent tries double it.
Nothing limits concurrent tries or checks free space first. Options are a size cap that
refuses with a message, or copy-on-write (`FICLONE` on filesystems that have it). This
belongs with § 4.2. **Decided and built (2026-10-31):** the cap, `limits.try-bytes` (512 MiB)
plus `limits.free-bytes` (045.Limits.md).

### 3.4 `changeset_list` parses every change set in full, under the change-set lock (medium)

`changesets.cpp:722`: a change set can hold 48 MiB (uploaded modules, content). The list
reads and parses each file to show its title and state, while holding `changesets_mu_`, which
`changeset_apply` also takes. It returns every row, with no limit. Change sets are never
deleted (§ 3.5). The fix is a small index (id, title, state, by, times) kept beside the files.

### 3.5 Things on disk that are never pruned (medium)

| what | where | kept |
| --- | --- | --- |
| change sets, drafts and discarded ones included | `operations/changesets/*.json` | forever, up to 48 MiB each |
| `tries` rows (which version was tried) | `operations/jobs.db` | forever, one row per box, version, tenant, and kind (small) |
| job runs | `operations/jobs.db` `runs` | forever (small: a cursor, 20 errors, 50 log lines each) |
| snapshots | `snapshots/<tenant>/` | until a person deletes them; at `limits.snapshot-files` (100) or `limits.snapshot-bytes` (2 GiB) per tenant, `db_snapshot`, `job_start`, `schema_apply`, and `changeset_apply` refuse until then; from 80% the snapshot's answer warns, and `/goober/ready`'s `limits` check lists the tenant (045) |
| stored box versions | `boxes/` | forever |

The snapshot limit is the one that bites: when it's reached, every operation that snapshots
first stops, and only the panel frees space. `host_health` doesn't show how near the limit a
tenant is. Panel idea for the other worker: per-tenant snapshot bytes and count against the
limit.

### 3.6 Live per-URI stats can be pushed out by a scan of a box's parameter routes (low)

`live_.record` (`host.cpp:738`) groups the host's 404s as "(not found)" and refused requests
as "(refused)". A box route with a parameter (say `/p/{id}`) that answers its own 404 is still
keyed by the raw path. A scan of `/p/<random>` then fills the LRU of 10,000 paths and pushes the
site's real pages out of the Performance view. The referer's path (`from`) is the visitor's to
choose too, so it can fill the flows LRU (20,000). Both are bounded, so this is a degraded view, not
memory. A fix would key a box's 4xx by its route pattern.

### 3.7 The disk check can block a deploy (low, by design)

A host under 256 MiB free fails `/goober/ready`, so a deploy then rolls back to the previous
build, which is no better off. That's the right signal, since the deploy's own copy of the
binary and the nightly need room. The deploy's output names the `disk` check.

## 4. For the human

### 4.1 Nobody is told when something is wrong

Every signal here (`/goober/ready`, `host_health`, the panel, the Errors and Box failures
views, `backup list`) is something a person must look at. Nothing pushes. A host can be
failing its backups, running out of disk, or answering 5xx all night, and the human learns of
it from a visitor. The options:

1. **Mail the operators.** The host mails `host:owner` when `/goober/ready` stays not-ok for
   10 minutes, when `disk` goes low, when a nightly is missed, or when a route's 5xx rate
   jumps. It's the cheapest, since the host already sends mail, but mail is also what's down
   when SES keys break.
2. **An outside checker.** UptimeRobot, Healthchecks.io, or a cron on another machine polls
   `/goober/ready`. It works when the host is down entirely, which option 1 can't.
3. **Both.** Recommended: option 2 for "is it up", option 1 for everything the host can see
   in itself.

### 4.2 How big may a try be? (decided: a cap, 045)

Tries on copies (§ 3.3) are the engineers' main safety practice. Either cap them (refuse a try
when the databases it would copy are over N MiB, or when free space is under twice that), or
build copy-on-write copies. The recommendation is a cap now (N = 512 MiB, plus the
free-space rule) and copy-on-write when a database first nears it.

The human, 2026-10-31: "What is Try? We should have a general rule about size limits." Built
as 045.Limits.md: every thing the host grows on its disk has a time limit and a byte budget,
set under host.json's `limits`. A try's copies are held to `limits.try-bytes` (512 MiB) and
must leave `limits.free-bytes` (1 GiB) free.

### 4.3 Retention for change sets and job history (decided)

Change sets (§ 3.5) are the undo record, so deleting them is a policy choice. The
recommendation is to keep applied and rolled-back ones for 400 days, as logs are kept, and to
delete drafts and discarded ones after 30 days.

**Decided 2026-10-31:** applied, failed, and rolled-back sets are kept 90 days after their last
change, drafts and discarded ones 30; built (042 § 9).
 Listed in 045's table.

### 4.4 Restore drills (postponed)

`just rehydrate` restores any moment onto a laptop, and `just backup-e2e` checks the format.
Nothing proves on a schedule that last night's real backup restores. A weekly
`temporal-daily.sh`-style job could rehydrate the newest nightly into a sandbox and check its
row counts against the live host's. It needs the backup keys on the machine that runs it,
which is the human's call. The human, 2026-10-31: postponed until a product is done.
