## 0. A word before we begin

Right then. I have been handed eleven documents, a bet on the future of computing, and a cheerful instruction to be useful. I shall try to be useful *and* cheerful, which is the British way, and also thorough, which is the machine way.

Let me say at the outset what kind of reviewer I am. I have no stake in the project's success, no feelings about Adama, and no allegiance to any vendor. I have read rather a lot of systems papers. I shall praise what deserves praise, name what can simply be bought, be frank about the limits, and then propose ways round them. I shall also assess the security posture, because the entire project is, at heart, a security argument dressed up as a platform.

Two goals frame everything that follows, and the author set them, not I:

1. **Rebuild Hearth** ([adama-platform/hearth](https://github.com/adama-platform/hearth)) in a more generic way. Hearth is the author's single-machine, single-jar, "one person's infrastructure" server, with AI as its interface.
2. **Then build a blue-collar operating system:** tools to run trades, small businesses, and their websites, able to operate **1,000 distinct products that never really need to be updated.**

I shall judge the design against both. Splendid. Let us get on with it.

## 1. The design in one breath

For readers arriving cold, here is the whole thing compressed. Every clause is cited.

Goober is a bet that agents will write most software, that agents cannot be trusted with the old keys, and that the answer is "better boxes, with finer scope", making "the wrong action impossible rather than merely frowned upon" [000 §Introduction]. A **box** is an ephemeral WASM process with no POSIX inheritance [000 §The Foundational Thinking]. It is named by a primary key and is dead until a **manifest** declares its routes, data, limits, and protocols [000 §The Foundational Thinking].

The manifest is JSON and is "the primary artifact that humans read" [010 §The Manifest]. Routes are keyed by protocol, then path, then verb, with a tooling-managed **signal** linking host and guest [010 §Start with Hello World]. The manifest and the WASM module deploy atomically through a **human approval queue** [010 §Start with Hello World]. The C++ host generates a strongly typed C ABI using the **WASM reactor pattern**: `goober_on_start` runs once per isolate, then `goober_on_request` (sync) or `goober_on_event` (async) runs per request or event, and the event loop always lives in the host [010 §Start with Hello World].

**Behaviour** is set by three fields. `boundary` (sync or async) chooses the shape of networking. `lifecycle` (solo or shared) chooses whether an isolate outlives a request. A per-isolate **budget** bounds a shared isolate, and a drain bounded by `timeout-ms` follows it [010 §Start with Hello World]. Requests with different resolved scopes or claims never share an isolate [010 §Start with Hello World].

**Data** is reached only through **strategies** (`data.$provider.$strategy`) built on well-known access patterns (`by_id`, `by_index`), with scope resolved from server-held **context** and never from the caller [020 §Data Flow; §Where context comes from]. Generated functions take *no id argument at all* [020 §Generated code]. Writes accumulate in a host-held **write set** that commits atomically [025 §Batched writes and no torn writes]. Network side effects ride along in an **outbox** [035 §The Outbox]. Real-time is a **bus of monotonic signals, never data** [040 §Why the last write is all that matters]. HTTP, JSON types, and mail are converted into typed values by the host, so the box never touches a protocol byte [036, 037, 038]. Around all of it, commodity infrastructure is rented so that the host stays small enough to read [099 §Keeping the host small].

If I had to put the thesis in nine words, they would be the author's own: "we don't frown upon the wrong action, we remove the words for it" [030 §Bringing in a service].

## 2. What is genuinely unique

Much of the design is a clever arrangement of known parts, and there is no shame in that; most good systems are. But a handful of ideas are, to my knowledge, either new or newly *combined* in a way I have not seen shipped as one coherent product. These are the crown jewels. Guard them.

### 2.1 Removing the words, not checking the sentence

The single most distinctive move is that generated interfaces **omit the parameters that would let a box misbehave**. Four instances:

- `user_by_id_read(struct users_row* out)` has no id, because the id comes from context [020 §Generated code].
- `mailer_send_message` has no `to` field when `require` pins `body.to` to `{user_email}` [030 §Bringing in a service].
- Reply addresses are minted and verified by the host, so a box never names a ticket it was not handed [038 §What makes this special].
- Redirects are declared, not built, so open redirects are impossible [037 §The status vocabulary].

Most capability systems check a request against a policy at call time. Goober instead shapes the *vocabulary* so that the forbidden request cannot be spoken at all. That is a meaningful difference. A policy can be misconfigured, but an absent parameter cannot be misused. I have seen fragments of this idea in object-capability languages and in row-level security, but not carried consistently through data, network, mail, and HTTP the way these documents carry it.

### 2.2 Isolate sharing keyed on the resolved data signature

The rule that requests may share an isolate **only when their resolved scopes and claims are identical** [010 §Start with Hello World] is made concrete in two places:

- **Data:** "every context value that is visible to the box is part of the signature" [020 §Where context comes from].
- **Network:** "a box that can only email its caller is a box that holds one caller at a time" [030 §Consequences for the box].

The result is a principled bridge between per-request isolation (safe, expensive) and pooled workers (cheap, leaky). Edge platforms pool isolates by deployment, not by data scope. Keying reuse on the *authority* a request carries is, as far as I know, original. It also gives one key for four jobs: the same signature drives isolate reuse, cache invalidation [025 §Cache coherency and sequencers], subscription deduplication [040 §The quadratic problem], and ETags [037 §What the host does for free]. That kind of economy is how one recognises a good abstraction.

### 2.3 A checker that predicts behaviour before deployment

The manifest checker:

- computes a route's worst case from `by_id` and `by_index` limits, call budgets, write sets, timeouts, and sequencer placement [025 §Can a box even behave well?];
- reports exactly where torn writes are possible [025 §Where torn writes are possible];
- refuses `shared` alongside SQL in the box [020 §SQL and solo];
- classifies type changes as safe or breaking, and names the boxes a breaking change affects [036 §Changing a type].

Static analysis of *the perimeter* rather than the code is a lovely inversion. The author has accepted that the interior may be slop and has insisted that the *shape of the worst it can do* is knowable [025 §Can a box even behave well?]. I have not seen a platform publish a "torn-write report" per route. It is a genuinely good idea and should be built early, because it is the thing a human reviewer will actually read.

### 2.4 A pub/sub that refuses to carry data

Pub/sub designs usually die of delivery guarantees. This one survives by giving them up:

- signals carry only "topic is now at sequence N" [040 §Why the last write is all that matters];
- signals coalesce per subscriber [040 §The quadratic problem];
- a signal is published if and only if its write set commits [040 §The manifest];
- subscription evaluations are deduplicated per signature, with the host refusing writes during evaluation [040 §The quadratic problem].

Reactive-query systems exist (I shall name one shortly), but tying publication to the *same* commit as the data and the outbox, so that data, side effects, and signals form one atomic unit, is tidy and, in my judgement, under-appreciated.

### 2.5 Email as a first-class, capability-bearing protocol

Mail routes as described are unusually sharp:

- addresses act as paths [038 §Converting mail into routes];
- the box's verdict becomes the SMTP verdict, so there is no backscatter [038 §What makes this special];
- only DMARC-aligned senders become principals [038 §What makes this special];
- signed `reply+{ticket : token}` addresses act as capabilities [038 §What makes this special].

Most platforms treat inbound mail as an afterthought webhook. Treating an email address as a bearer capability with host-verified claims is the sort of thing that makes support desks, quoting tools, and job-site coordination much safer. That matters enormously for the blue-collar goal (§7).

### 2.6 An honest separation between machine and human

The approval queue for any change to a box's shape [010 §Start with Hello World; 030 §Bringing in a service; 036 §Changing a type] is not unique by itself. Combined with the documentary discipline of this very repository (human docs authoritative, machine docs proposals), though, it forms a governance model the author describes in the intro: agents may *deny* a manifest change, but only humans may *approve* one [000 §The Foundational Thinking]. That asymmetry is wise, and I would keep it sacred.

## 3. What can simply be bought

A god-like machine must be candid, so here is the candour: **a great deal of this exists commercially or in open source**, sometimes in a remarkably similar shape. Knowing this is not discouraging. It tells the author where to spend originality and where to rent.

| goober concept | closest existing thing | how close it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| WASM box with a manifest declaring outbound hosts, key/value stores, and SQLite access | **Fermyon Spin** (`spin.toml` per component with allowed outbound hosts and declared stores) | Very close in spirit for the perimeter. Lacks the scope-from-context data model and the generated "missing words" ABI. |
| Capability-linked WASM components | **wasmCloud** (capability providers, links, WIT interfaces) | Close on capabilities and links. Different on data scoping and approval. |
| Edge isolates, per-object single-writer storage, queues, cron, email | **Cloudflare Workers** with Durable Objects, D1, Queues, Cron Triggers, and Email Workers | The most complete commercial analogue. Durable Objects are effectively "one sequencer per scope" [025 §Cache coherency and sequencers], and Email Workers can reject at SMTP time [038 §What makes this special]. Not self-hostable, and authority is not expressed as a reviewable perimeter. |
| Scope resolved from verified token claims | **Supabase / PostgreSQL row-level security** | The same idea as `principal/*` context [020 §Where context comes from], enforced as policy rather than as missing parameters. |
| Reactive queries re-run when data changes | **Convex** | Very close to the bus's "subscription that polls a function" [040 §The Bus], with deterministic queries and transactional mutations. |
| Single-binary SQLite app with auth and realtime over SSE | **PocketBase** | Close to Hearth's "one jar, one directory" ethos and to the SSE subscriptions [040 §The manifest]. A pragmatic reference for the blue-collar target. |
| Per-tenant SQLite at scale | **Turso / libSQL**, **LiteFS**, **Litestream** | Directly cited by the design [025 §Second, data that embeds in a machine, packs up, and goes away]. Buy, don't build. |
| Outbox and durable side effects | **Temporal**, **Inngest**, or a transactional outbox table | The design's outbox-in-the-same-file [035 §Starting small] is the simpler, better choice for SQLite, but the delivery machinery is standard. |
| Policy as data | **Cedar**, **Open Policy Agent** | Useful if `require` ever grows "ranges, sets, and patterns" [030 §Starting small]. Borrow the semantics before inventing a language, which the author rightly fears [030 §Starting small]. |
| Typed interfaces across languages | **WebAssembly Component Model / WIT** | A ready-made, multi-language replacement for the hand-generated C ABI [010 §Start with Hello World]. See §5.3. |
| Edge, mail, bus, backups, supervision | **Caddy, Stalwart, NATS, Litestream, systemd** | Already rented by design [099 §Keeping the host small]. |

**My verdict:** the *runtime* is largely buyable, and the *perimeter-as-vocabulary* is not. Sixty to seventy per cent of the host could be assembled from Wasmtime, Spin-like plumbing, SQLite, Litestream, NATS, and Caddy. The irreducible originality lives in:

- the manifest semantics,
- the generator that removes words,
- the signature-keyed sharing,
- the checker's predictions, and
- the approval workflow.

Spend the author's precious attention there, and rent the rest without a flicker of guilt.

## 4. Limits of the design

Now the frank part. None of these limits is fatal. Several are simply unwritten chapters. I list them roughly from most to least consequential for the two goals.

### 4.1 Identity is assumed, never designed

Everything rests on verified principals: `principal/agent` and `principal/email` [020 §Where context comes from; 030 §Bringing in a service], claims in the sharing rule [010 §Start with Hello World], and aligned mail senders [038 §What makes this special]. Yet there is **no design for who verifies a token, how a user logs in, how sessions work, or how an agent is delegated authority.** Hearth answers this with MCP plus OAuth, with agents acting "as the person who connected it". Goober must answer it too, or the whole scope model floats on air.

### 4.2 Time does not exist

There are no timers, schedules, or cron-like routes anywhere in the corpus. Hearth sends a 6am daily email. Every blue-collar business runs on reminders, recurring jobs, follow-ups, and invoices that fall due. The intro promises that "time is a boundary too, and we make it a first-class citizen" [000 §The Foundational Thinking], but no document delivers on it beyond time ranges in data [025 §Second, data that embeds in a machine, packs up, and goes away].

### 4.3 There is no user interface story

Boxes respond with text, JSON, or empty bodies [010 §Start with Hello World; 037 §Everything a request can carry]. Hearth serves websites, templates, versioned pages, redirects, and dynamic pages. A blue-collar operating system is mostly forms, schedules, quotes, and a public website. Static assets, templating, server-rendered HTML, and file uploads are absent, apart from mail attachments in object storage [038 §What makes this special].

### 4.4 Query expressiveness is deliberately thin

`by_id` and `by_index` are the whole data vocabulary for now [020 §SQL and solo]. That is a fine start, as "every ORM starts" there [020 §SQL and solo]. But real products need range queries, search, aggregates (this month's revenue), and joins (jobs with their customer). The escape hatch is SQL, which forces solo and query inspection, or human-approved stored procedures [020 §SQL and solo]. That will be hit early and often.

### 4.5 "Never needs updating" collides with expand-and-contract

The migration story is expand-and-contract, with contraction only once "no deployed manifest references the old version" [025 §First, structured data that can version and live migrate]. That is correct, but it implies that **old boxes must eventually be rebuilt** to let the platform contract. That contradicts the goal of 1,000 products that are never updated. Something must serve old shapes forever, or the goal must be softened.

### 4.6 External APIs drift, and boxes pin them

Each external service is coupled to a pinned OpenAPI document, and a changed document changes the box's shape [030 §Bringing in a service]. Third parties *will* change their APIs: payment processors, SMS gateways, fitness APIs. Under the current design, each such change forces a box update through the approval queue, which collides with the "never updated" goal.

### 4.7 The approval queue does not scale to a thousand products

Human approval of every shape change [010 §Start with Hello World] is the governance backbone. At one product it is a virtue. At a thousand products, each with tenants, types [036 §Changing a type], external documents [030 §Bringing in a service], and schemas [025 §First, structured data that can version and live migrate], it becomes a queue no human can drain. The design needs to say which changes are cheap enough to approve automatically.

### 4.8 Atomicity is narrower than it first appears

A route stays atomic only if its writes stay within one database and one sequencer [025 §How atomic is atomic?], and there is "one sequencer per scope" [025 §Cache coherency and sequencers]. If scope means the *resolved strategy key*, then a single request touching `user_by_id(7)` and a tenant-wide orders table already spans two sequencers. Under the stated rules, that makes many ordinary routes non-atomic. The granularity of "scope" for sequencing is underspecified, and the choice matters a great deal (see §5.8).

### 4.9 Credentials are per box, not per person

Credentials are a name like `secrets/mailer`, attached by the host [030 §Bringing in a service]. Hearth's gym feature uses *each user's* Hevy API key. Many blue-collar integrations (a tradesperson's own accounting software, their own calendar) are per user or per tenant. The design has no way to template credentials from context.

### 4.10 User-supplied URLs have nowhere to live

External bases are fixed in the manifest [030 §Bringing in a service]. Hearth fetches **user-provided ICS calendar links** to compute availability. That is a fetch to a URL the *user* chose, which is exactly the case the network design does not cover, and it is a classic server-side request forgery (SSRF) vector.

### 4.11 The trusted computing base is written in C++

Boxes are sandboxed in WASM, but the host is "written in C++" [010 §The Manifest]. The host parses HTTP (behind Caddy), JSON (validation and serialization [036 §Where a type is defined]), and builds MIME and DKIM signatures [038 §Sending mail]. It holds every secret [030 §Bringing in a service] and every lease [099 §Stickiness is for speed, not correctness]. The least trusted code is in the strongest jail, and the most trusted code is in the least memory-safe language. That irony deserves a decision, not a shrug.

### 4.12 C is an awkward language for agents to write

Starting with C to "establish a reasonable estimate of the overhead of copying data" [010 §The Manifest] is a sound measurement choice. As the *authoring* language for a thousand products written by agents, though, it invites memory bugs. WASM contains them, but they still manifest as "elevated error rates" [000 §The Foundational Thinking]. The intro already anticipates JavaScript via V8 [000 §The Foundational Thinking], and Hearth's dynamic pages run in V8.

### 4.13 Observability is deferred

Instrumentation is postponed to "a development flow design" [010 §Start with Hello World]. Telemetry is a data-flow path in its own right. Without scoping rules, detailed failure accounting becomes a side door around the data boundary (see §6.4).

### 4.14 Small inconsistencies that a precision project should not tolerate

These are minor, but the author has asked for extreme precision, so here they are:

- **Two type vocabularies.** Query parameters use `"type": "bool"` [037 §Everything a request can carry], while the types document uses JSON Schema `boolean` [036 §A strict subset]. Context uses `"type": "int64"` [020 §Data Flow], while JSON Schema uses `integer` with `format: int64` [036 §A strict subset]. Pick one vocabulary.
- **The 030 mail example mixes up its layers.** The service is labelled `mail_user`, but the generated function is `mailer_send_message`. An `operation` field sits at service level, yet operationIds belong to methods [030 §Bringing in a service].
- **Magic signal numbers.** Examples hard-code `signal == 42` even though tooling may renumber signals [010 §Start with Hello World]. Generated constants are needed.
- **The 010 schema table is incomplete.** It lists only `"text"` for `response` [010 §Start with Hello World], while later documents use `json`, `json:Order`, and `empty` [020 §Data Flow; 037 §Everything a request can carry].
- **The `token` parameter type is undefined.** Mail addresses use `{ticket : token}` [038 §Converting mail into routes], but `token` appears in no type table.
- **A missing document.** "The database migration design" is referenced [020 §Data Flow] but does not yet exist.

## 5. Proposals to overcome the limits

Here is where I get to be constructive, which is my favourite bit. Each proposal names the limit it addresses.

### 5.1 An identity and delegation design (for §4.1)

Write `045.Identity.md`. I recommend the following:

- **The host is the relying party.** Caddy terminates TLS, and the host verifies OIDC or OAuth tokens and session cookies. Their claims become `principal/*` context [020 §Where context comes from].
- **Agents act on delegated principals.** An agent connection, through MCP with OAuth as Hearth does it, yields `principal/agent` claims that *name the connecting human*. That gives agents exactly the authority of the person who connected them, and no more.
- **Every agent action is audited.** Extend the egress audit [030 §Consequences for the box] to an *ingress* audit of agent calls, with arguments and results, much as Hearth keeps its last 1,000 AI actions.
- **Return 404, never 403, on scope misses.** Hearth's principle that "a 403 confirms what is behind" belongs in the status vocabulary [037 §The status vocabulary].

### 5.2 MCP as a protocol (for §4.1 and the Hearth goal)

Add `mcp` beside `http`, `sse`, and `mail` under `routes` [010 §Start with Hello World; 037 §Other protocols speak HTTP too]. Each tool is a route. Its input and output schemas come from the types document [036 §Where a type is defined], and its descriptions come from the manifest's "mission" metadata [000 §The Foundational Thinking]. The agent's principal is resolved by the host. Since boxes already become OpenAPI documents for free [036 §Why borrow OpenAPI], generating MCP tool lists is the same trick again. This makes "AI as the interface" a property of *every* goober product, not a bespoke feature.

### 5.3 Use the Component Model for the ABI (for §4.12)

Keep the C measurements, but have the generator emit **WIT interfaces** and let the Component Model produce bindings for C, Rust, JavaScript (via ComponentizeJS or a V8 box), Go, and others. The generated structs [036 §A strict subset] map naturally onto WIT records, and resources map onto handles such as attachments [038 §What makes this special]. Reactor exports [010 §Start with Hello World] become component exports. The "missing words" property survives intact, because the *generator* still decides which parameters exist.

### 5.4 Timers as routes (for §4.2)

Add a `timer` protocol:

```json
{
	"routes": {
		"timer": {
			"daily-digest": {
				"fire": {
					"name": "daily_digest",
					"schedule": "0 6 * * *",
					"zone": "{tenant_timezone}",
					"for-each": "lookup/active_users"
				}
			}
		}
	}
}
```

The host fires a request per resolved context, with its own write set and outbox [025 §Batched writes and no torn writes; 035 §The Outbox]. Missed fires are caught up idempotently, keyed by the schedule instant, and the checker multiplies `for-each` cardinality by the route's worst case [025 §Can a box even behave well?]. Time becomes, at last, the first-class citizen the intro promised [000 §The Foundational Thinking].

### 5.5 Pages, assets, and uploads (for §4.3)

- **Pages provider.** A content-addressed store of static assets plus templates, rendered by the host from the box's typed response, so the box returns `struct invoice` and the host renders HTML. That extends "the box never writes protocol bytes" [037 §The invariants] to HTML, which incidentally removes cross-site scripting from the box's vocabulary.
- **Uploads.** Treat multipart bodies like mail attachments: the host scans, sizes, and stores them in object storage, and the box receives handles [038 §What makes this special].
- **Proposals, not actions.** Generalise Hearth's "moving a page proposes a redirect" into a host feature: risky changes to a tenant's content become proposals that a human accepts. It is the approval queue's little sibling [010 §Start with Hello World].

### 5.6 More access patterns, each with a cost ceiling (for §4.4)

Before reaching for SQL, add patterns the checker can still bound [025 §Can a box even behave well?]:

| pattern | example | ceiling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `by_range` | jobs between two dates | an index plus `limit` |
| `search` | customers matching "smith" | SQLite FTS5 plus `limit` |
| `aggregate` | revenue by month | a declared view, refreshed on commit |
| `join_one` | job with its customer | a declared foreign key, one row per row |
| `append` | an audit log | write-only |

Declared views fit naturally with sequencers: a view refreshes on commit and publishes its own topic [040 §The manifest].

### 5.7 Compatibility views, so old boxes never need rebuilding (for §4.5)

Because the host executes every generated query [020 §Patterns, tenants, and limits], it can **serve old schema versions through views**. A box pinned to `core13.schema` [020 §Data Flow] keeps reading and writing a `core13` projection of a `core17` table. The platform can contract the *physical* schema whenever it likes. The *logical* schema a deployed manifest sees is frozen forever, as long as a mapping exists, and the checker refuses migrations that make an old projection impossible. This turns the expand-and-contract invariant [025 §First, structured data that can version and live migrate] from "rebuild old boxes" into "write the mapping once".

### 5.8 Sequence per database file, not per key (for §4.8)

Define the sequencer's scope as **the database file**: for per-tenant data, the tenant [020 §Patterns, tenants, and limits]. Then every write set within one tenant is a single SQLite transaction with one sequence, which matches the "one writer per file" lease exactly [025 §Second, data that embeds in a machine, packs up, and goes away]. Cache invalidation can stay precise per resolved key [025 §Cache coherency and sequencers], since invalidation and ordering need not share granularity. Most blue-collar routes then stay atomic for free.

### 5.9 Host-side adapters for external drift (for §4.6)

Let a box keep its pinned OpenAPI document forever [030 §Bringing in a service]. When the real service changes, the operator ships a **host-side adapter** that maps the old document to the new API. That is an anti-corruption layer, reviewed once and shared by every box that pinned the old document. The box never learns the world moved. The same pattern covers SMTP and TLS churn, which the edge already absorbs [099 §Keeping the host small].

### 5.10 Credentials templated from context (for §4.9)

Allow `"credentials": "secrets/hevy/{user_id}"`. The host resolves the secret per principal at call time, the credential still never enters the box [030 §Bringing in a service], and the resolved secret path joins the signature [020 §Where context comes from].

### 5.11 A fetch capability for user-supplied URLs (for §4.10)

Add an `external` kind, `fetch`, with:

- a URL that comes from data, not code,
- a scheme allowlist (https and webcal),
- refusal of private, loopback, and link-local addresses after DNS resolution, with the resolved address pinned,
- size and time caps, and
- a host-side cache (Hearth caches calendars for one hour).

It must be read-only, with `effect: "none"` [035 §Safe calls and effectful calls].

### 5.12 Risk-tiered approval (for §4.7)

The checker already classifies changes [036 §Changing a type; 025 §Where torn writes are possible]. Turn that into tiers:

- **Green:** additive types, new read-only routes in an existing scope. Auto-approved and logged.
- **Amber:** new writes, new external methods, new effectful calls. Approved in batches by a human.
- **Red:** new scopes, new credentials, `immediate` effects, `atomic: false`, SQL in the box. Approved individually, with the torn-write and worst-case reports attached [025 §Can a box even behave well?].

The human then reviews the perimeter, never the product, which is precisely the intro's promise [000 §The Foundational Thinking].

### 5.13 Shrink or harden the trusted computing base (for §4.11)

Either write the host in a memory-safe language (Rust is the natural partner for Wasmtime), or keep C++ but **move parsing out**:

- Caddy handles HTTP framing [099 §Keeping the host small].
- A small, fuzzed parser handles the strict JSON subset [036 §A strict subset].
- Stalwart builds MIME and signs DKIM, rather than the host [038 §Sending mail].

Then fuzz every boundary continuously. Whichever route is chosen, the host's size budget should be written down as a number.

### 5.14 One vocabulary of types (for §4.14)

Make the JSON Schema subset [036 §A strict subset] the *only* type vocabulary. Path parameters, query parameters, context, and tokens all become schema references, and `{id : int64}` becomes shorthand for `{type: integer, format: int64}`. Define `token` as a host-verified signed string with declared claims.

## 6. Security evaluation

Now for the bit the whole design exists to win. I shall go through each adversary, give a judgement, and then list recommendations.

### 6.1 A malicious or buggy box (the primary threat)

**Strengths, which are considerable:**

- **No ambient authority.** There are no sockets [030 §The Network] and no database connections [020 §Patterns, tenants, and limits]. Credentials never enter the box [030 §Bringing in a service].
- **No parameters for what it must not do.** There is no id [020 §Generated code], no recipient [030 §Bringing in a service], and no redirect target [037 §The status vocabulary].
- **Contained resources.** Each request has a timeout [010 §Start with Hello World]. Shared isolates have per-isolate budgets [010 §Start with Hello World], and calls have per-request budgets [030 §Consequences for the box]. Arrays need `maxItems` and objects are closed [036 §A strict subset].
- **Isolation by scope.** Requests with different resolved scopes or claims never share an isolate [010 §Start with Hello World]. Every context value the box can see is part of the signature [020 §Where context comes from].
- **Atomic damage.** A trap throws away the write set [025 §Batched writes and no torn writes], and side effects stay queued until commit [035 §The invariants].

**Gaps:**

- **Covert channels between isolates on one host.** These include CPU timing, cache side channels, and shared-runtime state. They are low risk for blue-collar data, but worth naming. Mitigations are process-per-tenant pools for sensitive scopes and fuel metering to blunt timing.
- **Deduplicated subscription output can leak.** If a box's response depends on something outside its signature, such as time or randomness, dedup is harmless. But if the signature ever misses a visible value, one evaluation is broadcast to many viewers [040 §The quadratic problem]. The "every visible context value" rule [020 §Where context comes from] must be enforced by construction: the box should only ever *receive* context through the signature.
- **Results routes are a new ingress.** Outbox result routes [035 §What the box sees] are requests the world triggers. They must carry the original request's context, frozen, rather than a fresh one.

**Rating:** strong. This is the best-defended surface in the design.

### 6.2 A malicious tenant user

**Strengths:** scope comes from verified claims, not URLs [020 §Where context comes from]. `route/*` context is limited to public, read-only strategies [020 §Where context comes from]. Invalid input never wakes the box [037 §The invariants], and CORS is set by the manifest [037 §What the host does for free].

**Gaps:**

- **Authentication itself is undesigned (§4.1).** Every strength above inherits that hole.
- **Denial of wallet.** Budgets are per request and per isolate [010 §Start with Hello World; 030 §Consequences for the box], but there are no per-tenant, per-day, or per-principal quotas. A user hammering an endpoint that calls a paid SMS API passes every per-request check.
- **Client retries duplicate side effects.** Idempotency keys come from "the request and the element's position" [035 §Delivery], so a client that retries a POST creates a *new* request and a second email or charge. Accept a client `Idempotency-Key` at the HTTP layer [037 §Everything a request can carry] and fold it into the outbox key.

**Rating:** good in principle, provisional in practice until identity exists.

### 6.3 An external attacker on the network or the mail path

**Strengths:**

- The edge is rented and hardened (Caddy, Stalwart) [099 §Keeping the host small].
- SPF, DKIM, and DMARC checks happen before a sender becomes a principal [038 §What makes this special].
- Rejections happen at the door, so there is no backscatter [038 §What makes this special].
- Loops die at the host [038 §What makes this special].
- Reply tokens are signed [038 §What makes this special].

**Gaps:**

- **The MTA hook endpoint must be authenticated.** If anything other than Stalwart can POST to the host's mail endpoint, an attacker can inject "verified" senders. Use mutual TLS or a shared secret, and listen on loopback or a private interface only.
- **Trust between Caddy and the host.** The host must refuse requests that do not come from Caddy, and must ignore client-supplied forwarding headers.
- **Reply tokens can be forwarded.** A token in an address travels wherever the email is forwarded, so anyone on the thread holds the capability. Bind tokens to the intended sender's *aligned* identity where possible, give them expiry, and support rotation and revocation of the signing key.
- **User-supplied URLs open SSRF (§4.10).** See proposal 5.11.

**Rating:** good, with three concrete fixes required.

### 6.4 An operator, an insider, or a compromised piece of infrastructure

**Strengths:** humans approve shape changes [010 §Start with Hello World]. Secrets live in a single place, the host [030 §Bringing in a service]. Every egress is audited [030 §Consequences for the box].

**Gaps:**

- **Backups have a huge blast radius.** Litestream streams every tenant's file to object storage [025 §Second, data that embeds in a machine, packs up, and goes away; 099 §Keeping the host small]. Whoever holds the bucket credentials holds everyone's data. Encrypt each tenant's file with its own key before upload, use write-only credentials on hosts, and require restores to go through a separate privileged path.
- **Telemetry routes around the data boundary.** "Detailed accounting for the reasons failures happened" that an agent can "tap … in real time" [010 §Start with Hello World] will contain request data. Telemetry should inherit the data boundary of the request it came from. Agents should get aggregates by default, and scoped access to detail only with an audited grant.
- **The approval queue invites social engineering.** In a world where "words are viruses" [000 §Introduction], an agent can write a persuasive manifest "mission" to win approval. The approval UI should lead with the checker's mechanical reports (scopes, effects, torn writes, worst cases), and put prose descriptions *last*.
- **The checker is part of the trusted computing base.** A bug in the checker silently approves the unsafe. It deserves the same scrutiny as the host: tests generated from adversarial manifests, and ideally a small formal core.

**Rating:** adequate today, and needs the three fixes above before multi-tenant production.

### 6.5 Supply chain

The box binary and its manifest deploy atomically [010 §Start with Hello World], which is excellent. Box binaries should also be **content-addressed and signed** by the build, and the host should refuse unsigned modules. Pinned OpenAPI documents [030 §Bringing in a service] and types documents [036 §Where a type is defined] should be hashed into the manifest's identity. The host's own dependencies, which include Wasmtime, SQLite, and a JSON library, should be pinned and audited. A single host CVE is a fleet-wide incident by construction.

### 6.6 The ten things I would do first for security

1. Design identity and delegation (§5.1) before any multi-user deployment.
2. Authenticate every internal hop: Caddy to host, Stalwart to host, and host to NATS.
3. Add per-tenant and per-principal quotas, alongside the per-request budgets.
4. Accept client idempotency keys and fold them into outbox keys.
5. Encrypt each tenant's backups with its own key, and use write-only credentials for Litestream.
6. Scope telemetry by the data boundary, and make detailed access an audited grant.
7. Reply tokens: expiry, rotation, revocation, and binding to aligned senders where possible.
8. A `fetch` capability with private-address blocking for user-supplied URLs.
9. A memory-safe host, or a host with all parsing moved out and continuous fuzzing.
10. Signed, content-addressed modules and hashed schema documents in the manifest's identity.

**Overall security judgement:** the *architecture* is unusually strong. Removing authority from the vocabulary is the right primitive, and the documents apply it with admirable consistency. The *implementation surface* around the architecture is mostly unwritten: identity, internal trust, telemetry, backups, and quotas. That is the right way round. It is far easier to bolt sound operations onto a sound perimeter than the reverse.

## 7. Goal one: Hearth, rebuilt generically

Hearth is one person's server: one jar, one directory, with AI as the interface. Goober is a platform for many boxes. The generic rebuild means expressing each Hearth feature as **an ordinary goober box plus host capabilities**, so that Hearth becomes the *first product* rather than a special-purpose server. Here is the mapping.

| Hearth feature | goober construct | what's missing today |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One H2 file per domain | Per-tenant SQLite with `"database": "tenant_{tenant_id}"` and `lookup/tenant_by_domain` [020 §Patterns, tenants, and limits] | Nothing. An exact match. |
| Copying a directory as backup | Litestream to object storage [025 §Second, data that embeds in a machine, packs up, and goes away; 099 §Keeping the host small] | Per-tenant encryption (§6.4). |
| Dynamic pages in V8 with a one-second ceiling | A solo box with the default `timeout-ms` of 1000 [010 §Start with Hello World] | A V8 box type [000 §The Foundational Thinking] and the pages provider (§5.5). |
| Versioned pages, templates, and proposed redirects | Pages provider plus host-level proposals (§5.5) | Not designed. |
| Gym via the Hevy API, acting as you | External OpenAPI service with `require` [030 §Bringing in a service] | Per-user credentials (§5.10). |
| Ranch tasks as state machines, and graduating habits | Data strategies scoped by `principal/*` [020 §Where context comes from], with the logic in the box | Nothing structural. |
| Daily 6am email, suppressed when empty | Timer route (§5.4), then the outbox [035 §The Outbox] and Stalwart [038 §Sending mail] | Timers. |
| Votes with ICS availability, cached for an hour | A `fetch` capability (§5.11) plus a host cache | `fetch`. |
| Mail forwarding rules with SPF, DKIM, DMARC, SRS, and ARC | Mail routes via Stalwart [038 §Converting mail into routes] | SRS and ARC belong in Stalwart and its configuration, not in a box. |
| Per-user mailboxes over IMAP | Stalwart's native IMAP and JMAP [099 §Keeping the host small] | Buy, don't build. |
| Calendar subscription with a token hashed at rest | A route with a `token` capability parameter [038 §What makes this special] | `token` as a general type (§5.14). |
| MCP with OAuth, agents acting as the connecting user | `mcp` protocol routes plus `principal/agent` [020 §Where context comes from] | Identity and MCP (§5.1, §5.2). |
| An AI log of the last 1,000 actions | The egress audit [030 §Consequences for the box], extended to ingress | Ingress audit (§5.1). |
| Eleven permissions | Claims in context [010 §Start with Hello World] | A role and claim design (§5.1). |
| 404 rather than 403 | The status vocabulary [037 §The status vocabulary] | One sentence to add. |
| 1,460 integration tests against a live server | Black-box testing at the boundary: shadow traffic and staging per agent [000 §The Foundational Thinking; 010 §Start with Hello World] | Contract tests generated from manifests (§8.4). |

**Reading the table:** about half of Hearth maps onto designs that already exist. The rest needs **five host capabilities**: identity with MCP, timers, `fetch`, pages and uploads, and per-principal credentials. Build those five, and Hearth becomes roughly a dozen small boxes, each with a manifest a human can review in a minute. That review is the whole point [000 §Introduction].

The ethos transfers nicely, too. Hearth's refusals ("never rank a feed, never move money, never track anybody") can become **platform-level manifest lints**. A manifest that declares a payment processor's effectful methods could, for example, require a red-tier approval (§5.12). Values become checker rules, which is exactly the "governance" move the intro calls for [000 §The Foundational Thinking].

## 8. Goal two: a blue-collar operating system for 1,000 products that never need updating

This is the ambitious bit, and I confess it delights me. Let us be precise about what "never really need to be updated" must mean, and then design for it.

### 8.1 What "never updated" means

A product, meaning a manifest plus a WASM module plus its types and schema documents, is **immutable once approved**. It continues to work, securely, for years, while everything around it changes: TLS versions, mail standards, browser behaviour, third-party APIs, host internals, storage engines, and hardware. Updating a product becomes something one *chooses* for new features, not something one is *forced into* for maintenance.

### 8.2 Why goober can plausibly deliver it

Most software rots because it owns things that change. Goober's boxes own almost nothing:

- **No protocol code.** HTTP, JSON, MIME, and SMTP live in the host and edge [037 §The invariants; 038 §The invariants].
- **No security-sensitive parsing.** It happens before the box wakes [037 §What the host does for free].
- **No credentials** [030 §Bringing in a service], **no sockets** [030 §The Network], and **no storage engine** [020 §Patterns, tenants, and limits].
- **No operational concerns.** Migrations, replication, caching, batching, retries, and durability belong to the host [025 §What the box never thinks about; 040 §What the box never thinks about].

The box owns only *business logic over typed values*. Business logic for a plumber's booking form does not rot. The design's central bet doubles as an anti-rot strategy, and that may be its most commercially valuable property.

### 8.3 What must be added to make it true

1. **A frozen, versioned ABI.** Every host import is versioned (`goober_v1_*`), and the host supports every ABI version it has ever shipped, the way some operating systems keep old binaries running for decades. The Component Model with versioned WIT packages (§5.3) makes this tractable.
2. **Compatibility views for schemas** (§5.7). Old boxes see old shapes forever.
3. **Host-side adapters for external APIs** (§5.9). Old boxes see old APIs forever.
4. **Immutable, signed, content-addressed artifacts** (§6.5). "Version" means "hash", which Hearth already embraces: "no version numbers; commit is identity".
5. **Wide time types.** Absolute timestamps must be `int64` [036 §A strict subset]. Durations like `time-ms` can stay `int32` [010 §Start with Hello World], but nothing absolute should, or a product will meet 2038 rather abruptly.
6. **A conformance corpus per product.** At approval time, the platform records representative traffic and outcomes per route, using the shadow and staging machinery [000 §The Foundational Thinking]. Every *host* release replays all 1,000 corpora. The fleet is the host's test suite. That is how one changes the host weekly while never touching a product.

### 8.4 How to build a thousand products without writing a thousand products

Most blue-collar products are recombinations of perhaps twenty **blocks**, each one a box:

| block | used by |
| --- | --- |
| Booking and scheduling | plumbers, cleaners, salons, mobile mechanics |
| Quotes and estimates | builders, landscapers, roofers, decorators |
| Invoices and payments (outbox to a payment processor) | everyone |
| Customer records | everyone |
| Job sheets with photos (uploads) | trades, inspections, farm work |
| Reminders and follow-ups (timers) | everyone |
| Inbound mail and SMS triage (mail routes) | everyone |
| Public website and pages | everyone |
| Reviews and referrals | everyone |
| Inventory and stock | food trucks, farm shops, small retail |

A product is then a manifest that **composes blocks through box-to-box calls** [030 §Boxes talking to boxes; 035 §Boxes talking to boxes, again], with product-specific types [036 §Where a type is defined], copy, and a small amount of glue logic. Agents write the glue, the checker bounds it [025 §Can a box even behave well?], and humans approve the perimeter (§5.12). One thousand products becomes roughly twenty blocks plus a thousand small compositions. Each composition can be reviewed in minutes because it *is* its manifest.

### 8.5 Operating them

- **Tenancy:** one SQLite file per tenant per product [020 §Patterns, tenants, and limits], which places, moves, and deletes cleanly [025 §Second, data that embeds in a machine, packs up, and goes away]. Ten thousand small businesses is a few hundred thousand small files, which SQLite, Litestream, and a filesystem handle comfortably.
- **Density:** solo by default [010 §Start with Hello World] costs one isolate per request. With precompiled modules and a pooling allocator, instantiation in Wasmtime is cheap. Shared lifecycles [010 §Start with Hello World] are reserved for heavy `goober_on_start` work.
- **Cost:** every box is metered by budgets and calls [030 §Consequences for the box], so per-product, per-tenant cost is known, which makes pricing a small business plan straightforward.
- **Failure:** a bad product shows up as "elevated error rates and latency for a project", not a breach [000 §The Foundational Thinking].
- **Stickiness and placement:** these follow tenants [099 §Stickiness is for speed, not correctness], and one machine is a real deployment [099 §One machine, then many]. A regional blue-collar operating system could plausibly run on a handful of servers.

### 8.6 What the blue-collar domain specifically demands

- **Money.** Unlike Hearth, this domain *must* move money. Do it only through effectful external calls that ride the outbox, with client idempotency keys [035 §Delivery; §6.2]. Accept payment-processor webhooks as a signed inbound protocol verified by the host, like mail senders [038 §What makes this special].
- **Phones.** SMS and voice deserve the mail treatment: inbound messages as routes and outbound messages through the outbox. Phone numbers act as aligned principals where the carrier attests them, and reply links act as capabilities.
- **Offline work.** Job sites lack signal. Here the bus's design shines: a client that reconnects just asks for the current state [040 §Why the last write is all that matters]. Offline *writes* would want an append-only pattern [040 §What the box never thinks about] with server-side reconciliation.
- **Plain language.** The users are tradespeople, not engineers. Manifest "mission" metadata [000 §The Foundational Thinking] plus MCP (§5.2) means a plumber can run the product by talking to it, which is perhaps the most transformative possibility in this entire document.

## 9. A suggested order of work

1. **Host skeleton:** Caddy plus a single host serving hello world from a reactor box [010 §Start with Hello World; 099 §One machine, then many]. Measure C copy overhead, as intended [010 §The Manifest].
2. **The checker, early:** worst-case and torn-write reports from day one [025 §Can a box even behave well?]. They are the product a human reviewer actually uses.
3. **Data plus the write set:** per-tenant SQLite, `by_id` and `by_index`, atomic commit, and the outbox table in the same file [020; 025; 035 §Starting small].
4. **Identity plus MCP** (§5.1, §5.2).
5. **Timers, `fetch`, pages and uploads, and per-principal credentials** (§5.4, §5.5, §5.10, §5.11).
6. **Hearth on goober** (§7), as the first real product and the proof.
7. **Compatibility views, adapters, a frozen ABI, and conformance corpora** (§8.3), to make "never updated" true.
8. **Blocks, then compositions** (§8.4): the blue-collar catalogue.

---

## 10. Closing remarks

I have read a great many design documents that promise to tame complexity, and most tame it by hiding it. These documents tame it by **removing authority from the vocabulary**, which is rarer, harder, and much more durable. The consistency is the impressive part: the same move recurs through signals, data, network, mail, HTTP, types, and the bus. The author warned that the work "is a personal research bet, written so it can be argued with" [000 §Introduction]. Very well. I have argued, cheerfully, and on balance I find the bet sound.

The limits are real: identity, time, interface, query breadth, and a trusted base in C++. Every one of them can be met *within* the design's own philosophy rather than against it, which is the surest sign that a philosophy is worth keeping. If the five missing host capabilities are built and the "never updated" machinery is taken seriously, Hearth on goober is a matter of months, and a thousand dull, durable, delightful products for people who fix boilers and mow lawns is a matter of years.

Jolly good work. Carry on.
