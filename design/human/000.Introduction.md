The SlopBox Orchestration Service

# Introduction

We live in the era of AI, and this project is an itch to scratch and a bet on how computing ends up. This bet is made while the work can still pay for itself or just entertain me. It is foolish to wait on a god-level AI, and we build from reasonable predictions of the capability curve in front of us. If the god shows up, nothing in this document matters, and that is a clean way for it to die.
The assumption under that bet is narrow and more optimistic than the doomer script. Sure, capability gets steep, cheap, and available. A cheap easy button lands inside every organization, and it feels like magic because, for a while, it is. It is also a direct stress test on the old trust model. Current idioms smuggle in a quiet premise: the worker is a human, and the human can be trusted with the keys. There are incentives and disincentives that can be managed. Humans can be yelled at, shamed, praised, and loved; agents can't. Agents set that premise on fire. Can we trust them? I do not know. I am betting we should not hand them the old keys. Give them better boxes, with finer scope, and make the wrong action impossible rather than merely frowned upon.
So the aim is not a human reading all the code since we are already proudly running in that direction. The moment I stopped reading code was a day of sadness for me as a craftsman. It is also not an audit of every artifact. In this project, we build tooling that drives quality by what a person can see and feel quickly. Ironclad proofs sit on the boundary. A human should be able to feel the shape of a box, feel the risks of that box going wrong, and act in a way to adjust.
The operating environment around those boxes stays sane, monitored, and clean. Cost control is part of the contract, not a lecture or a promise, since we control the fuel in and out of a box. Liability is already under threat from predictions that the machine will do something unbound, and mistakes will happen. The perimeter exists so the shape of a mistake stays inside a contract, and so we can know whether a failure was even possible. Trust the agent with one user profile. It cannot open another profile, cheat, and most of all is unable to delete the universe. In a world where words are viruses, the map has to be a sane artifact a person can audit.
Read this specification as a new operating system. Every existing idiom is up for grabs, and it still has to run on systems that exist today. That is how the work gets explored in pieces without pretending the future is free. Problem boundaries that earned their keep, like SQLite or HTTP, are treated as good shapes. The embodiment of a shape is disposable, and that is the reading contract. Some ideas do not go away because they are good and rooted deeply in science that is hard to vibe an alternative to. The people that crafted those implementations around science and craft have the benefit that their artifacts can be carried forward, and we seek to reward people in this new world to craft new artifacts as well.
A note for anyone who already knows the corpses of my life. This is not Adama with a new coat of paint. I spent five years there, and I am robbing my own grave for the parts that still hold. The rest of this document is a personal research bet, written so it can be argued with. If it is only nostalgia, then that is OK as well. I put it in public such that I can stake a flag on the shape of the future.
# The Foundational Thinking
We have come a long way from tape, and the UNIX model has endured greatly because the ideas under it were good enough to survive contact with every generation that tried to replace them. Once it gave birth to containers, we started to go on the right path, not because containers were magic but because they finally drew a line between an interior you could throw away and a perimeter you could keep. The history of all of this is a story of incremental progress that you can have your AI walk you through, and I am not going to retell it here, but I believe the ideas are good at the core and worth paying homage to before we start inventing. What should be respected is the boundary that earned its keep. What is up for invention is the embodiment that only survived because building a better one used to be too expensive to attempt.
What has changed with agents doing the implementation work is the level of paranoia required, because the agent is free of status, survival needs, obligations, and violence, and the old levers do not move it. Humans can be yelled at, shamed, praised, and loved, and there is a whole craft of leadership built on that fact, but agents cannot, and yet the old-world obligations of liability and violence still land on the people outside the box. Your beliefs about where AI is good or not depend on how serious the work is, and we fully expect that line to move over time, so the engineering move is to treat the shift as an engineering problem rather than a mood. The fashionable, trendy, popular, and human concerns can be pulled down into token counts, guardrails, precision, and formalisms, and the point of doing that is governance: many of these ideas are not new, the past deserves respect, and the future is still a threat. This section is here to walk the thinking away from magic, name what the past already got right, and put a common vocabulary back on the table before the design starts.
An unstated opportunity with AI, and with the legion of PhD interns it brings and the stamina those interns do not run out of, is that inventors are no longer tethered to the old cost function that killed momentum before a new idea could be tasted. Building used to be the expensive part, so we inherited embodiments we did not love because replacing them would have consumed the decade. That tether is loose now. New ideas are cheaper to build, cheaper to get a taste for, and then cheaper to execute at scale if the taste was right, which means the scarce things are quality and paranoia rather than the hours required to find out you were wrong. The field does not become mature because the model is clever. It becomes mature when the wrong action is hard to take and a human can feel that before the bill arrives.
We start with both storage and compute because the division is cheaper than fusing them into a self-updating model, and a look at the energy cost of these models against the machines we already run is enough to see why. We acknowledge there is a future where storage and compute become one inside a self-updating model universe, but it is doubtful that fusion will be as reliable as the division we have already invented, and the reliability and predictability of these fixed-function machines will stay cheaper than what a model spends to imitate them. Thus the division remains, because even a god-like AI will use spreadsheets, not out of nostalgia but because a god-like AI would still respect how those idioms function at massive scale, and that is just how good some of what we already built turned out to be.
Storage sits above the block, because the block is a shape we already respect and do not need to invent again, and what we want on top of it is a set of multi-paradigm engines treated as first-class citizens. That choice mirrors a mathematical universe an agent can work with, reason about, and drive at a higher level, and we do it to spend fewer tokens and to lean on the shape of the training data instead of asking the model to rediscover tables, documents, and keys on every job. We keep the hierarchical nature of storage, with pathing conventions like storage/sqlite3/$database/$version/$table, so a human can read the address and a machine can enforce it. The walk starts at a global namespace, drops into a provider, and then drops again into whatever scope that provider actually knows how to give, and from there the path resolves into a conventional access policy. The provider owns everything beneath it and supplies the scoping we need while data is in flight. Time is a boundary too, and we make it a first-class citizen rather than a column someone remembered to add.
SQLite is really good, so it is the tactically smart way to move, and it hands us a database with tables, which is an idiom strong enough to leverage instead of replace. Taking SQLite also means taking the operational burden that comes with it, including replication, schema management, schema migration, versioning, and data migration, and that alone is a comprehensive package. Thank the lord we have an army of infinite-stamina agents to do the heavy lifting. This is also how the trust equation gets balanced, because the plumbing has to be well defined and a human still has to review it in the micro, and the micro scope stays narrow, lean, and dense in a mathematical sense. We are not asking a person to read the product. We are asking them to read the pipe.
A process wakes up somehow, does a thing, and goes to bed. I explored the other dream heavily in Adama, a living process whose code could change while the memory stayed, and that dream has dramatic requirements that force too much chaos into everything around it. The process model here stays ephemeral in nature. We can still think differently about how long a process lives and whether it is portable, and many of these ideas are not new, but they are not equitable across platforms, which is why they have to be named again instead of assumed. We start with an empty box such that the process and the container are the same thing. The success in my career was putting chaotic product code into boxes, because boxes simplify the architecture and contain the chaos, and that is the exact goal of putting guardrails in place for these models. For a clean mental model we start with a WASM process that has no existing standard runtime and no POSIX inheritance, because we are rethinking the POSIX model from the ground up. WASM is hyper-specific, and it is still a valid tactical entry we can execute today and that can reasonably last while other process models evolve in shape and form. The key is a box that runs some code in extreme isolation.
We can grow past WASM. JavaScript via V8 is a box. A well-defined container the system operates like a static-file web server is a box. We start with the most complex form and also accept that we will want extremely common and predictable boxes, so the system can optimize around known lines for higher performance instead of treating every interior as a surprise. Because the process is extremely isolated, the entrance to the box becomes a common architecture concern rather than a per-product invention. We start with HTTP so the process is reachable, which is a reasonable way to start it and interact with it, and from there we can add a terminal-style entrance, bi-directional pipes, and other interaction idioms as they earn their place. This is a philosophy of where validation lives. Reviewing an abstract HTTP server made by an agent is a task an expert with deep experience can actually do, and that same expert will be lost in a product of a thousand features, but there is evidence that humans can operate at the abstraction boundary. Agents do not need to reinvent an HTTP server for every product. They can carry the burden of the thousand features, and the entrance stays a thing we already know how to look at.
Five arenas have to be contended with before the micro design means anything: naming the box, data flowing into the box, data flowing out of the box, interior effects of the code, and process multiplicity and lifetime. Naming starts with a single primary key, /process/wasm/the-process-name, which is the name of the container along with the runtime, and it is fundamentally useless beyond changing what is inside the box. The next step is a manifest with routing rules. Those rules map paths and protocol covenants between the process and the ecosystem, and we borrow heavily from the file system and from HTTP because the internet is already a good model for addressable content that is dynamic. /manifest/wasm/the-process-name is a structured file the system knows, and it carries the metadata around the process, including an alias listing of paths and the protocol that process supports. An alias can say that /path/to/process/{parameter : int64} speaks request/response, with JSON in and JSON out. Protocols live in the orchestration layer so the checking can be system-wide. We embrace more than stdin and stdout, and we want those channels formalized. HTTP is the tactical start, and other bi-directional protocols can follow, along with reliable or unreliable transports, because the point is that things can talk to each other and have their interface checked, monitored, and managed. Tactically the whole machine, or the whole cluster, becomes a server.
Boxes have primary keys and routes that address them through those protocols, and in terms of data a box is dead until the manifest contains its requirements. The manifest says which data sources, or which other processes, are required, and those requirements can be scoped by the parameters on the aliased route. That creates a thin scope and places invariants on the topology of the data the box is allowed to process. It mirrors the authorization claim something like IAM already solves, except here it is enforced, and we can specify consumption limits per data requirement. Session or context variables live in the same manifest, including how a request looks them up. A tenant identifier can arrive that way and become the means of selecting a specific database and table. The rule is to keep the manifest simple and rule-based, so it can carry an extremely fine governance model around access and around what gets generated to feed the box.
For a human using the system, this is very annoying. For an AI generating the system, it is great, and it simplifies the human review of what a box does. The manifest can carry metadata about the mission of what goes in the box, because we are thinking agent-first, which means absolute discipline first and human review and sign-off as the shippable step. Agents can even be given the capability to deny a change to the manifest while still requiring a human to approve one, so we maximize agent usage without handing them the keys to the perimeter. In the same vein the manifest shows where the box can write and what limits it has. Beyond making bad side effects impossible, that gives the box fuel, and it gives us the ability to monitor the entire box as a visual diagram. We can feel the code and constrain the topology. Operationally that means default rate limits per input or output, a cost we can measure per box, and a way to rate-limit how boxes impact the fleet.
Legally, this can handle most of what scares people at scale: audits, liability, and blast radius. The quality of what is inside the box stays an open question, but the tools to probe it and play the black box are available in a form that other tools can meaningfully work with and monitor. The goal is the same class of guarantee enterprise governance already asks for, plus the audit, logs, and monitoring that let machines and humans work together. By taking the boundary, we can isolate, fork, shadow traffic, A/B test, and test fully, using a full range of black-box quality controls across all boxes. That gives agents new abilities to test their own work at integration and end to end, and by taking control of the data we can stand up automatic staging at the per-agent level so an agent can navigate the product in a meaningful way instead of guessing at a system it cannot see.
The interior still has notes worth keeping in view. If we use C inside the box to make the box go brr, we do not have to care about memory management as a product concern in the same way, because we can monitor it from outside. We think about what happens between requests, and about request multiplicity. The worker model suggests spinning up an isolate per request, and we can also do fast swaps to cut the cost of that isolate, and we can play with state and even elect leader boxes that live for a period of time. This is a known game, so we start with a worker model and experiment with a shared worker that has a deadline. The operating environment handles many catastrophic concerns of poorly engineered quality inside the box, and it produces metrics of that quality which can be managed per the task domain. Every resource gets fuel limits and timeouts. What we experience, when the interior is bad, is elevated error rates and latency for a project, not a box that opens another profile, cheats, or deletes the universe.
